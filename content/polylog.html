Almost all orbits, with a prescribed target and height bound

How far does a typical Collatz orbit fall, how long does it take, and how high can it climb first? This paper answers all three questions together in ordinary natural density.

Write \(T(n)=n/2\) for even \(n\), and \(T(n)=(3n+1)/2\) for odd \(n\). The standard map \(\operatorname{Col}\) uses \(3n+1\) at odd inputs instead. A shortcut odd step combines two standard steps, so the same landing value can have different times in the two maps.

Theorem 1.1 · manuscript v4.0.2

Choose any positive functions \(U(n),R(n)\to\infty\), as slowly as desired. For a set of starting integers of natural density one, there are nonnegative integers \(k,\ell\) with

\[T^k(n)=\operatorname{Col}^{\ell}(n)<(\log n)^{A_{\rm FP}}U(n),\] \[|k-c_*\log n|+|\ell-\tfrac32c_*\log n|\le K\sqrt{\log n\,\log\log n},\] \[\max_{0\le j\le k}T^j(n)\le nR(n),\qquad \max_{0\le i\le\ell}\operatorname{Col}^{i}(n)\le nR(n).\]

Here \(A_{\rm FP}\approx9.9911\), \(c_*=2/\log(4/3)\approx6.9521\), and \(K\) depends on the chosen functions. The conclusions hold for sufficiently large members of that density-one set; neither function needs to be monotone.

The target multiplier \(U\) and height multiplier \(R\) can be chosen independently. Taking both to be the same function \(f\) gives the statement in the abstract: descent below \((\log n)^{A_{\rm FP}}f(n)\), with both preceding orbits bounded by \(nf(n)\).

“Natural density one” means that the proportion of successful starts among \(1,\ldots,X\) tends to one as \(X\to\infty\). It allows an exceptional set; it does not resolve any particular exceptional orbit.

A fixed exponent gives an explicit exceptional rate

For every fixed \(A>A_{\rm FP}\), Corollary 1.2 sets

\[q=\frac{A}{2A_{\rm FP}}-\frac12>0.\]

All but at most \(C_A X/(\log X)^q\) integers up to sufficiently large \(X\) satisfy the joint conclusions with target \((\log n)^A\), the same centered clocks, and both orbit heights at most \(n(\log n)^q\). For example, \(A=2A_{\rm FP}\) gives \(q=1/2\).

The order of this joint exceptional count is sharp because of the height requirement (Proposition 7.1). It is not a lower bound for failure to descend when no height bound is required.

Why the proof follows first passages

The multipliers of the shortcut map are approximately \(1/2\) and \(3/2\). Giving the two parities equal weight suggests a mean logarithmic decrease of \(\tfrac12\log(4/3)\), whose reciprocal is \(c_*\). This explains the leading clock, but a drift heuristic alone cannot be restarted after each descent: the landing values need not be uniformly distributed.

  1. Encode finite parity words exactly. Each word determines an affine iterate on its residue class, including the additive Collatz terms.
  2. Follow first passages through a decreasing threshold chain. Later bad passages are counted among the original starting integers, keeping the arithmetic dependence between stages.
  3. Compress the possible cumulative times. In a dyadic shell \([2^M,2^{M+1})\), only \(O(\sqrt{M\log M})\) passage times need to be counted at each threshold.
  4. Use a finite survival estimate. This supplies the additional saving that permits the critical exponent with arbitrary divergent multipliers.

The constants come from \(p_*=\log_3 2\), the binary entropy \(H_2\), and \(\kappa_*=1-H_2(p_*)\), with \(A_{\rm FP}=1/(2\kappa_*)\). The written proof and the formalization give the precise estimates behind these steps.

A fixed multiple of \((\log n)^{A_{\rm FP}}\) is outside Theorem 1.1: the multiplier must tend to infinity. The paper does not prove the pointwise Collatz conjecture.

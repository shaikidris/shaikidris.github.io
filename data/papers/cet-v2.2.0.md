## Abstract

Natural-density iteration faces a transport problem: block endpoints are
arithmetically generated, not uniform. For the shortcut Collatz map
\[
T(n)=
\begin{cases}
n/2,&n\text{ even},\\
(3n+1)/2,&n\text{ odd},
\end{cases}
\]
let
\(F(n)=T^{\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor}(n)\) be its logarithmic-block endpoint map.
We prove an arbitrary-target theorem: a power-saving exceptional-set bound
pulls back under \(F\) with exponent asymptotically linear in the input
exponent. Exact parity coordinates and fixed-total compositions reduce
endpoint multiplicities to a Rényi estimate; Hölder's inequality combines it
with target mass.

As an application, put \(a_0=(\log_2 3)/2\),
\(\delta_0=\log(1/a_0)/\log(2/a_0)=0.251245530155874\ldots\), and
\(c_*=2/\log(4/3)\). For every \(0<\delta<\delta_0\), every
\(0<\sigma<1-\delta/\delta_0\), and every \(c>c_*\), the number of
\(n\leq X\) for which no \(k<c\log n\) satisfies
\[
T^k(n)\leq\exp((\log n)^{1-\delta})
\]
is at most \(5X\exp(-a(\log X)^\sigma)\) for some \(a>0\) and all
sufficiently large \(X\). Consequently natural density one holds below every
fixed power, with \(c=6.953\) admissible. Fixed-power natural-density theorems,
Tao's logarithmic-density theorem, and stronger Tao-based natural-density
bridges are comparisons, not inputs to this independent operator proof. This
is an almost-all result and does not prove the Collatz conjecture.

## 1. Introduction

The Collatz conjecture asks whether every positive integer eventually reaches
\(1\) under the map \(T\) above. This paper addresses a different and weaker
question: how small a value can be proved to occur for almost every initial
integer, when "almost every" means ordinary natural density?

Natural-density descent separates two questions that the full conjecture
combines: how deeply a typical orbit can be forced to descend, and whether
every orbit terminates. Replacing a known threshold by a smaller one measures
deeper certified descent while retaining ordinary counting density. It does
not, by itself, say anything about every individual orbit.

The main obstruction is iteration. After one descent block, the landing values
are generated by the orbit and need not be uniformly distributed in their new
shell, so a one-block density estimate cannot simply be applied again. This
paper isolates that obstruction as an operator problem: transport a
power-saving estimate for an arbitrary target set backward through one
logarithmic-block endpoint map.

Throughout, \(\log\) denotes the natural logarithm; logarithms to another
base carry a subscript. For \(S\subseteq\mathbb N_{\geq1}\), write
\[
B_S(X)=\#\{1\leq n\leq X:n\notin S\}.
\]
Also write \(T_{\min}(n)=\min_{k\geq0}T^k(n)\) for the least value attained
by the orbit of \(n\).
The set \(S\) has natural density one when \(B_S(X)=o(X)\). Earlier
natural-density results in the fixed-power line include the theorem of
Allouche [2], who proved
\(T_{\min}(n)\leq n^\theta\) for every fixed
\(\theta>3/2-(\log2)/(\log3)\); Korec [5] improved the range to
\(\theta>\log_4 3\); and Inselmann [4] obtained every fixed exponent
\(\theta>0\). The theorem below gives one prescribed threshold smaller than
every fixed power, together with a quantitative exceptional count and an
explicit logarithmic witnessing time.

At a different density level, Tao [8] proved descent below every function
tending to infinity in logarithmic density. Concurrent preprints of
Allikvere [1] and Mazur [6] obtain natural-density conclusions with arbitrary
diverging thresholds through bridges built on Tao's framework. Their terminal
threshold class is more general than the application below. These results,
and the fixed-power theorems above, provide context rather than proof inputs:
no theorem from them is invoked in the argument.

The distinct result here is a self-contained quantitative pullback theorem for
arbitrary target sets. Its proof converts exact parity words into compositions
with a fixed total, controls the resulting endpoint fibers by a Rényi moment,
and uses Hölder's inequality to transport the target estimate. Theorem 1.1 is
the principal descent application: it combines that reusable operator with an
endpoint-only bootstrap to give a prescribed stretched-logarithmic threshold,
a counting-cutoff estimate, and a logarithmic witnessing time.

Set
\[
\begin{aligned}
a_0&=\frac{\log_2 3}{2},
&\qquad
\delta_0&=
\frac{\log(1/a_0)}{\log(2/a_0)}
 =0.251245530155874\ldots,\\
c_*&=\frac2{\log(4/3)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1.1}
\]
The endpoint \(\delta_0\) is a supremum of the strict parameter ranges used
in the proof; equality is not asserted.

### Theorem 1.1

Fix \(0<\delta<\delta_0\).

1. The set of \(n\) satisfying
   \[
   T_{\min}(n)\leq\exp((\log n)^{1-\delta})
   \tag{1.2}
   \]
   has natural density one.

2. More quantitatively, for every
   \(0<\sigma<1-\delta/\delta_0\) and every \(c>c_*\), there are constants
   \(a_{\delta,\sigma,c}>0\) and \(X_{\delta,\sigma,c}\) such that,
   for every \(X\geq X_{\delta,\sigma,c}\),
   \[
   \begin{aligned}
   &\#\bigl\{1\leq n\leq X:\nexists k\in\mathbb N_0\text{ such that}\\
   &\hspace{38mm} k<c\log n\ \text{and}\
   T^k(n)\leq\exp((\log n)^{1-\delta})\bigr\}\\
   &\hspace{20mm}\leq
   5X\exp\!\left(-a_{\delta,\sigma,c}(\log X)^\sigma\right).
   \end{aligned}
   \tag{1.3}
   \]

Since
\[
c_*=6.952118993564416\ldots<6.953,
\tag{1.4}
\]
the numerical clock \(6.953\log n\) is an immediate instance of part 2.

The constants in (1.3) are effective in the sense that they are obtained
from the fixed parameters and finite maxima in the proof. They are not
optimized, and no practical numerical value of \(X_{\delta,\sigma,c}\) is
claimed.

The restriction on \(\sigma\) records the exponent budget left after repeated
endpoint pullbacks. Each block contracts the orbit scale while weakening the
available density exponent, and the number of blocks must still be large
enough to reach the prescribed stretched-logarithmic target. Section 7
balances these two requirements.

Theorem 1.1 is an almost-all theorem. It neither proves descent for every
initial value nor excludes exceptional cycles or divergent trajectories.
Section 8.1 records the precise scope of the conclusion.

### 1.1. Why logarithmic blocks appear

Suppose a block contains \(M\) half-Collatz steps and \(s\) odd steps. If the
additive terms are temporarily ignored, its multiplicative factor is
\[
\frac{3^s}{2^M}.
\]
For a typical parity word \(s\approx M/2\), so this factor is approximately
\[
\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)^M.
\]
Taking \(M\approx\log_2 n\) therefore sends the scale \(n\) to approximately
\(n^{a_0}\), where \(a_0=(\log_2 3)/2<1\). One logarithmic block gives a
fixed-power contraction. Repeating such blocks is what converts that
contraction into the stretched-logarithmic threshold in (1.2).

The choice of exactly \(M\) steps also matches the arithmetic of the shell:
its \(2^M\) starting values form a complete residue system modulo \(2^M\),
so Proposition 2.3 identifies them with all length-\(M\) parity words exactly
once. Shorter blocks retain exact uniformity but give less contraction, while
longer blocks no longer sample every parity word from one shell.

The difficulty is not the heuristic contraction. It is preserving a
quantitative natural-density estimate when the endpoint of one block becomes
the starting value of the next. The proof isolates this as a pullback problem.
For a set \(S\), define
\[
\operatorname{Pull}(S)
=
\{n\geq1:T^{\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor}(n)\in S\}.
\tag{1.5}
\]
The central technical result shows that a power-saving bound for the
complement of \(S\) survives this operation with an asymptotically linear
loss in its exponent.

### 1.2. Terminology

A dyadic shell is an interval \([2^M,2^{M+1})\); a logarithmic block starting
there consists of \(M\) iterations, and its endpoint is the terminal value
\(T^M(n)\). A cohort is a set of parity words with a specified odd count and,
when stated, a specified first odd position. *Fixed-total* means that the
composition total \(N\) is held fixed, while *central* means that its number
of parts lies within \(\varepsilon N\) of the mean \((N+1)/2\), for the fixed
\(\varepsilon\) in (4.6).

An endpoint fiber consists of starts sharing one endpoint. *Transport* refers
to passing a quantitative density estimate from a target set to its pullback
under the block endpoint map.

### 1.3. Proof outline

The proof has six steps.

1. **One block in parity coordinates.**  Parity words of length \(M\) are in
   bijection with the integers in a dyadic shell. The exact affine iterate
   expresses the block endpoint in terms of the parity word.

2. **Condition on the odd count.**  After fixing the number \(s\) of odd
   steps, a parity word becomes a positive composition of a fixed total.
   Equality of block endpoints is reduced to a congruence modulo \(3^s\).

3. **Control endpoint multiplicities.**  Words sharing one block endpoint form
   an endpoint fiber. Integer-lift injectivity and a quotient-order estimate
   convert congestion in its congruence class into a random variable measuring
   how much of the composition total is spent in its final parts. A Rényi
   bound at each fixed total controls its \((1+\theta)\)-moment when the number
   of parts is near its mean, for every fixed \(1/2<\theta<1\).

4. **Pull back a dense target.**  Regard the normalized endpoint
   multiplicity as a density on pairs consisting of an odd count and an
   endpoint. Hölder's inequality combines its Rényi moment with the mass of
   the bad target. This gives the small-\(D\) transport slope
   \[
   \Lambda(\theta)=a_0\frac{\theta}{1+\theta}.
   \tag{1.6}
   \]
   The factor \(\theta/(1+\theta)\) is the Hölder conjugate exponent, and
   the remaining factor \(a_0\) is the small-\(D\) slope of
   \(\psi(D)/\log2\).

5. **Iterate endpoints only.**  A terminal binomial-tail estimate supplies a
   fixed dense set on which one logarithmic block contracts \(n\) to at most
   \(3n^r\), for any fixed \(r>a_0\). The proof recursively pulls back this
   fixed contraction set; it does not track intermediate iterates inside a
   block. Requiring a shrinking envelope at every intermediate time would
   impose a stronger pathwise event not supplied by the endpoint pullback in
   Step 4. For an orbit-minimum theorem, endpoint iteration is the minimal
   recursion matching the conclusion.

6. **Choose the number of blocks.**  Using \(R\asymp\log\log n\) blocks gives
   endpoint size \(n^{r^R}\). The density recurrence remains summable exactly
   when the block-growth parameter lies between two explicit scalar bounds.
   Letting \(r\downarrow a_0\) and \(\theta\uparrow1\) gives (1.1).

This organization separates the two genuinely different tasks: proving a
one-block transport theorem and iterating its endpoint map. Every estimate is
stated in the form required by the next step.

### 1.4. Relation to other density results

The comparison above separates the strength of the terminal application from
the method developed here. The new quantitative mechanism is Theorem 5.3. In
the terminology of
Section 2.1, if \(S\) is \((C,D)\)-dense, then for fixed admissible
\(\theta,\varepsilon,\chi\), and all sufficiently small \(D\), it gives
\[
(C,D)\longmapsto
\left(
K\left(1+C^{\theta/(1+\theta)}\right)
D^{-\theta/(2(1+\theta))},\ \chi D
\right)
\]
under pullback. The constants are uniform in the target set, in \(C\), and in
the later values of \(D\). This arbitrary-target operator estimate, rather
than the particular descent set used in Section 6, is the reusable output of
the fixed-total analysis.

A later preprint by the present author [7] obtains stronger orbit-landing
conclusions through a different first-passage framework. Those conclusions do
not imply the arbitrary-target transport theorem above. The present paper is
therefore retained for that operator, its direct fixed-total proof, and the
quantitative descent application that illustrates it.

## 2. One logarithmic block

The proof begins on one dyadic shell, where a logarithmic block has a fixed
length. Two translations are needed before its endpoints can be counted.
First, shell estimates must be converted into estimates for all initial
intervals. Second, a starting integer must be replaced by its finite parity
word and the endpoint written exactly in those coordinates. Lemmas 2.1--2.2
handle the density and concentration estimates; Propositions 2.3--2.4 and
Corollary 2.5 provide the endpoint coordinates used in Section 3.

For reference, the recurring notation is grouped by its role in the proof.
The last column points to the defining display; symbols omitted from the table

\(\theta,\varepsilon,r,\chi,\zeta,\omega\) are chosen once and remain fixed

### 2.1. From dyadic shells to initial intervals

For \(C>0\) and \(0<D\leq1\), call a set
\(S\subseteq\mathbb N_{\geq1}\) **\((C,D)\)-dense** if
\[
B_S(X)\leq C X^{1-D}
\qquad(X\geq1).
\tag{2.1}
\]
Every such set has natural density one.

We need two elementary ways to pass from dyadic-shell estimates to (2.1).

### Lemma 2.1 (dyadic summation)

1. Suppose \(K>0\), \(0<\gamma<\log2\), and
   \[
   \#\bigl(S^c\cap[2^M,2^{M+1})\bigr)
   \leq K e^{-\gamma M}2^M
   \qquad(M\geq0).
   \tag{2.2}
   \]
   Then \(S\) is
   \[
   \left(
   \frac{2K}{2e^{-\gamma}-1},
   \frac{\gamma}{\log2}
   \right)\text{-dense}.
   \tag{2.3}
   \]

2. Suppose \(0<\sigma\leq1\), \(A,\gamma>0\), and, for all sufficiently
   large \(M\),
   \[
   \#\bigl(E\cap[2^M,2^{M+1})\bigr)
   \leq A e^{-\gamma(M+4)^\sigma}2^M.
   \tag{2.4}
   \]
   Then there are \(A',\gamma'>0\) and \(X_0\) such that
   \[
   \#(E\cap[1,X])
   \leq A'X\exp\left(-\gamma'(\log X)^\sigma\right)
   \qquad(X\geq X_0).
   \tag{2.5}
   \]
   One may take
   \[
   A'=2A+1,
   \qquad
   \gamma'=
   \min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{(4\log2)^\sigma},\frac12\right\}.
   \tag{2.6}
   \]

#### Proof

For the first assertion, let \(2^J\leq X<2^{J+1}\). Summing (2.2) over
\(0\leq M\leq J\) gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
B_S(X)
&\leq
K\sum_{M=0}^J(2e^{-\gamma})^M\\
&\leq
\frac{K(2e^{-\gamma})^{J+1}}{2e^{-\gamma}-1}
\leq
\frac{2K}{2e^{-\gamma}-1}
X^{1-\gamma/\log2},
\end{aligned}
\]
which is (2.3).

For the second assertion, choose \(M_*\) so that (2.4) holds for
\(M\geq M_*\), and take
\[
X_0\geq\max\{4,2^{2M_*}\}.
\]
When \(0<\sigma<1\), enlarge \(X_0\) so that
\[
\log X_0\geq(2\gamma')^{1/(1-\sigma)}.
\]
Again write \(2^J\leq X<2^{J+1}\). The shells with
\(M<\lfloor J/2\rfloor\) contain fewer than
\[
\sum_{M<\lfloor J/2\rfloor}2^M
<2^{\lfloor J/2\rfloor}\leq X^{1/2}
\tag{2.7}
\]
integers. On each remaining shell,
\[
M+4\geq\frac J2\geq\frac{\log X}{4\log2},
\]
so their total contribution is at most
\[
2AX\exp\left(-\gamma'(\log X)^\sigma\right).
\tag{2.8}
\]
The definition of \(X_0\) gives
\(\gamma'(\log X)^\sigma\leq\frac12\log X\); for \(\sigma=1\), this
follows from \(\gamma'\leq1/2\). Thus the right side of (2.7) is at most
\[
X\exp\left(-\gamma'(\log X)^\sigma\right).
\]
Adding (2.7) and (2.8) proves (2.5). \(\square\)

We shall repeatedly use three immediate consequences of (2.1):

- the intersection of \((C_i,D_i)\)-dense sets, \(i=1,2\), is
  \((C_1+C_2,\min(D_1,D_2))\)-dense;
- adding or deleting finitely many integers changes only the constant \(C\);
- if \(0<D'\leq D\), then every \((C,D)\)-dense set is also
  \((C,D')\)-dense.

The central-window argument also uses the following symmetric-binomial
estimate.

### Lemma 2.2 (symmetric-binomial tail)

If \(X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,1/2)\), \(n\geq1\), and \(y\geq0\), then
\[
\Pr\left(\left|X-\frac n2\right|\geq y\right)
\leq
2\exp\left(-\frac{2y^2}{n}\right).
\tag{2.9}
\]

#### Proof

For \(\lambda>0\),
\[
\mathbb E e^{\lambda(X-n/2)}
=
\left(\cosh\frac\lambda2\right)^n
\leq e^{n\lambda^2/8}.
\]
The last inequality follows from
\(\cosh u\leq e^{u^2/2}\): on \([0,\infty)\), the derivative of
\(u^2/2-\log\cosh u\) is \(u-\tanh u\), whose derivative is
\(\tanh^2u\geq0\). Markov's inequality with \(\lambda=4y/n\) gives the
upper tail. Applying the same argument to \(-X\) gives the lower tail.
\(\square\)

### 2.2. Parity words and exact endpoints

The finite parity-word coordinates are classical; see Terras [9] and
Everett [3]. We include the short proofs needed here.

For \(n\geq1\), define the parity bits and their prefix sums by
\[
p_i(n)=\mathbf 1_{\{T^i(n)\ {\rm odd}\}},
\qquad
s_k(n)=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}p_i(n).
\tag{2.10}
\]

Binomial concentration is useful only if parity words occur with exact, not
heuristic, uniformity among shell starts. The next proposition supplies that
identification.

### Proposition 2.3 (parity-word bijection)

For every \(M\geq0\), the map
\[
n\bmod 2^M
\longmapsto
\bigl(p_0(n),\ldots,p_{M-1}(n)\bigr)
\tag{2.11}
\]
is a bijection from \(\mathbb Z/2^M\mathbb Z\) to \(\{0,1\}^M\).
Consequently,
\[
\#\{n\in[2^M,2^{M+1}):s_M(n)=s\}
=\binom Ms.
\tag{2.12}
\]

#### Proof

The first \(M\) parity bits depend only on \(n\bmod2^M\), because
\[
n\equiv n'\pmod{2^{k+1}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
T(n)\equiv T(n')\pmod{2^k}.
\]
Conversely, the parity bit at time zero and the residue of \(T(n)\) determine
\(n\) one power of two more precisely. On the even branch,
\[
n=2T(n)\pmod{2^{k+1}},
\]
while on the odd branch,
\[
n=(2T(n)-1)3^{-1}\pmod{2^{k+1}},
\]
where \(3\) is a unit modulo \(2^{k+1}\). Induction recovers
\(n\bmod2^M\) from the parity word. The two finite sets have the same
cardinality, so the map is bijective. Each residue modulo \(2^M\) occurs
once in \([2^M,2^{M+1})\), and (2.12) follows. \(\square\)

Define an integer correction by
\[
c_0(n)=0,
\qquad
c_{k+1}(n)=
\begin{cases}
c_k(n),&p_k(n)=0,\\
3c_k(n)+2^k,&p_k(n)=1.
\end{cases}
\tag{2.13}
\]

The parity word specifies the multiplicative factor \(3^{s_k}/2^k\), but
the additive \(+1\) terms still have to be retained. The next identity keeps
that correction exact rather than replacing the Collatz block by its
multiplicative heuristic.

### Proposition 2.4 (affine iterate)

For every \(n\geq1\) and \(k\geq0\),
\[
2^kT^k(n)=3^{s_k(n)}n+c_k(n),
\tag{2.14}
\]
and
\[
0\leq c_k(n)<3^{s_k(n)}2^k.
\tag{2.15}
\]

#### Proof

Both statements follow by induction on \(k\). An even step leaves the
correction unchanged. At an odd step,
\[
c_{k+1}=3c_k+2^k,
\qquad
s_{k+1}=s_k+1.
\]
If (2.15) holds at time \(k\), then
\[
c_{k+1}
<3^{s_k+1}2^k+2^k
<3^{s_k+1}2^{k+1},
\]
because \(1<3^{s_k+1}\). This proves (2.14)--(2.15) simultaneously.
\(\square\)

For the fiber argument we shall not need a sharp bound on every correction.
The affine identity already gives the following uniform endpoint range on a
fixed shell and odd-count cohort.

### Corollary 2.5 (endpoint range)

If \(n\in[2^M,2^{M+1})\) and \(s_M(n)=s\), then
\[
0<T^M(n)<3^{s+1}.
\tag{2.16}
\]

#### Proof

Equations (2.14)--(2.15) and \(n<2^{M+1}\) give
\[
2^MT^M(n)
<
3^s2^{M+1}+3^s2^M
=3^{s+1}2^M.
\]
\(\square\)

## 3. Fixed-total endpoint fibers

Section 2 replaces every shell start by a parity word and expresses its block
endpoint through an integer correction. Pulling back a target set requires a
bound on how many words can share one endpoint. We condition on the number
\(s\) of odd letters, separate the first odd position, and encode the
remaining gaps by a positive composition of a fixed total. This turns the
endpoint-collision problem into a congruence-fiber problem modulo \(3^s\).

Fix a parity word \(w\in\{0,1\}^M\) with \(s\geq1\) odd letters. The first
lemma gives the exact dictionary between the word and its composition.

### Lemma 3.1 (parity words and compositions)

Let
\[
0\leq i_1<i_2<\cdots<i_s<M
\]
be the odd positions of \(w\). Put
\[
u=i_1,
\qquad
k_j=i_{j+1}-i_j\quad(1\leq j<s),
\qquad
k_s=M-i_s.
\tag{3.1}
\]
Then \(\mathbf k=(k_1,\ldots,k_s)\) is a positive composition of
\(N=M-u\). Conversely, \(u\geq0\) and a positive composition of \(M-u\)
into \(s\) parts recover a unique parity word through
\[
i_m=u+k_1+\cdots+k_{m-1}.
\tag{3.2}
\]
If \(c_w\) is the correction in (2.13), then
\[
c_w
=
2^u\sum_{m=1}^s
3^{s-m}2^{k_1+\cdots+k_{m-1}}.
\tag{3.3}
\]

#### Proof

The gaps in (3.1) are positive and sum to
\(M-u\). Formula (3.2) proves the inverse correspondence. Each odd step at
position \(i_m\) contributes \(2^{i_m}\) to the correction and is multiplied
by \(3\) at each of the \(s-m\) later odd steps. Hence
\[
c_w=\sum_{m=1}^s3^{s-m}2^{i_m},
\]
which becomes (3.3) after substituting (3.2). \(\square\)

For a positive composition
\[
\mathbf k=(k_1,\ldots,k_s),
\qquad
k_1+\cdots+k_s=N,
\]
put \(K_0=0\), \(K_j=k_1+\cdots+k_j\), and define
\[
\mathscr A_{\mathbf k}
=
\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}3^{s-1-j}2^{K_j}.
\tag{3.4}
\]
Thus (3.3) says \(c_w=2^u\mathscr A_{\mathbf k}\). Since \(M=u+N\),
with \(2^{-N}\) in the following congruence denoting the inverse of \(2^N\)
in \(\mathbb Z/3^s\mathbb Z\),
\[
2^{-M}c_w
=2^{-N}\mathscr A_{\mathbf k}\pmod{3^s}.
\tag{3.5}
\]

There are
\[
L_{s,N}=\binom{N-1}{s-1}
\tag{3.6}
\]
positive compositions of \(N\) into \(s\) parts. Let \(\nu_{s,N}\) be
the probability law of
\[
2^{-N}\mathscr A_{\mathbf k}\pmod{3^s}
\]
when \(\mathbf k\) is chosen uniformly from these compositions. For
\(\theta>0\), define
\[
\mathcal A_{s,N}(\theta)
=
3^{s\theta}
\sum_{a\bmod3^s}\nu_{s,N}(a)^{1+\theta}.
\tag{3.7}
\]
Because \(2^{-N}\) is a unit modulo \(3^s\), multiplication by it only
permutes residue classes. The same functional is therefore obtained from the
fibers of \(\mathscr A_{\mathbf k}\bmod3^s\).

The Rényi functional in (3.7) measures congestion in these residue fibers.
To control it by ordering integer representatives, we first need to know that
two different compositions never give the same integer correction.

### Lemma 3.2 (integer-lift injectivity)

For fixed \(s,N\), the map
\[
\mathbf k\longmapsto\mathscr A_{\mathbf k}\in\mathbb N
\]
is injective.

#### Proof

Dividing (3.4) by \(2^N\) gives
\[
2^{-N}\mathscr A_{\mathbf k}
=
3^{s-1}2^{-N}
+3^{s-2}2^{-(N-k_1)}
+\cdots
+2^{-k_s}.
\tag{3.8}
\]
Taking valuations in \(\mathbb Q_2\), the summands have valuations
\[
K_0-N<K_1-N<\cdots<K_{s-1}-N.
\]
Suppose two compositions give the same value in (3.8). Their first summands
are identical, so cancel them. The \(2\)-adic valuation of the remaining
sum is \(K_1-N\): its first coefficient is odd, and every later summand has
strictly larger valuation. Equality of the two remaining sums therefore
forces the two values of \(K_1\), and hence of \(k_1\), to agree. Cancel the
now identical next summands and repeat. This recovers
\(k_2,\ldots,k_{s-1}\); the common total \(N\) then recovers \(k_s\).
\(\square\)

Injectivity allows the values in each residue class to be ordered by their
quotients after division by \(3^s\). Distinct quotients must spread out, which
converts a large fiber into a large average correction.

### Lemma 3.3 (quotient-order estimate)

For every \(\theta>0\),
\[
\mathcal A_{s,N}(\theta)
\leq
(1+\theta)\,
\mathbb E_{s,N}
\left[
\left(
\frac{3^s+\mathscr A_{\mathbf k}}{L_{s,N}}
\right)^\theta
\right],
\tag{3.9}
\]
where \(\mathbb E_{s,N}\) denotes expectation under the uniform law on
positive compositions of \(N\) into \(s\) parts.

#### Proof

For \(a\in\{0,\ldots,3^s-1\}\), let \(f_a\) be the number of compositions
with \(\mathscr A_{\mathbf k}\equiv a\pmod{3^s}\). By Lemma 3.2, the
integer values in this fiber are distinct. Write them in increasing quotient
order as
\[
\mathscr A_i=a+3^sq_i,
\qquad
0\leq q_1<\cdots<q_{f_a}.
\]
Then \(q_i\geq i-1\), so
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f_a^{1+\theta}}{1+\theta}
&=\int_0^{f_a}x^\theta\,dx\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{f_a}i^\theta\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{f_a}(1+q_i)^\theta\\
&\leq
3^{-s\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{f_a}(3^s+\mathscr A_i)^\theta.
\end{aligned}
\]
The last inequality uses
\(3^{-s}(3^s+\mathscr A_i)=1+q_i+a/3^s\geq1+q_i\).
Multiply by \(3^{s\theta}\), sum over \(a\), and divide by
\(L_{s,N}^{1+\theta}\). This is (3.9). \(\square\)

Thus the arithmetic collision moment has been reduced to the expectation on
the right of (3.9). Section 4 estimates that expectation when the composition
length lies in the central range relevant to typical parity words.

## 4. The central fixed-total Rényi estimate

The quotient-order estimate leaves one probabilistic task: average the size
of the correction over compositions whose number of parts is close to half
the total. We work directly with a uniformly chosen set of separators of the
prescribed size. This keeps the total and the number of parts fixed, avoids a
conditioning loss, and makes the terminal composition blocks explicit. The
resulting moment is subcritical for \(\theta<1\) in a fixed central window.

Choose each of the \(N-1\) possible separators independently with
probability \(1/2\). This produces a uniform random positive composition of
\(N\). Its number of parts is
\[
J_N=1+\operatorname{Bin}(N-1,\tfrac12),
\qquad
p_{N,s}:=\Pr(J_N=s)
=\frac{L_{s,N}}{2^{N-1}}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

For \(0<\theta<1\), put
\[
\alpha_\theta=\frac{2^\theta-1}{3^\theta-1}.
\tag{4.2}
\]
Strict concavity of \(x^\theta\) at the midpoint \(2=(1+3)/2\) gives
\[
\frac12<\alpha_\theta.
\tag{4.3}
\]
We shall also need
\[
\alpha_\theta<\frac{\log2}{\log3}.
\tag{4.4}
\]
Indeed, the function
\[
x\longmapsto\frac{x^\theta-1}{\log x}
\]
is strictly increasing on \((1,\infty)\). After writing
\(z=\theta\log x\), its derivative has the sign of
\(e^z(z-1)+1\), whose derivative is \(ze^z>0\) and whose value at zero
is zero. Comparing \(x=2\) and \(x=3\) proves (4.4).

For \(1/2<\theta<1\), define the available central half-width
\[
\varepsilon_0(\theta)
=
\alpha_\theta-\frac12
=
\frac{2^\theta-1}{3^\theta-1}-\frac12>0
\tag{4.5}
\]
and, for \(0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0(\theta)\), set
\[
\mathcal I_{N,\varepsilon}
=
\left\{
s:\left|s-\frac{N+1}{2}\right|\leq\varepsilon N
\right\}.
\tag{4.6}
\]

The central half-width in (4.5) is chosen precisely so that the geometric
series over terminal composition parts used in the proof has ratio below one.
The next theorem is the only higher-moment input needed later.

### Theorem 4.1 (central fixed-total estimate)

Fix \(1/2<\theta<1\) and
\(0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0(\theta)\). There is a constant
\(C_{\theta,\varepsilon}<\infty\) such that, for every \(N\geq1\),
\[
\sum_{s\in\mathcal I_{N,\varepsilon}}
p_{N,s}\mathcal A_{s,N}(\theta)
\leq
C_{\theta,\varepsilon}N^{\theta/2}.
\tag{4.7}
\]

#### Proof

Fix \(N>2\) and \(1<s<N\). Under the uniform law on positive
compositions of \(N\) into \(s\) parts, the \(s-1\) separator positions
form a uniformly chosen subset of \(\{1,\ldots,N-1\}\). Let \(B_i\) be
its indicators and put
\[
\pi=\frac{s-1}{N-1},
\qquad
V_d=\sum_{i=N-d+1}^{N-1}B_i
\quad(1\leq d<N).
\tag{4.8}
\]
The sum defining \(V_1\) is empty and equals zero.
For a composition \(\mathbf k=(k_1,\ldots,k_s)\), reverse its parts and set
\[
U_r=k_{s-r+1}+\cdots+k_s,
\qquad
Z_{s,N}=\frac{3^s+\mathscr A_{\mathbf k}}{2^N}.
\tag{4.9}
\]
The separator at \(N-d\) occurs exactly when \(d\) is the total of a
nonempty proper terminal block. In that event \(V_d\) is the number of
separators strictly after it. Hence
\[
\sum_{r=1}^{s-1}3^{r-1}2^{-U_r}
=
\sum_{d=1}^{N-1}B_{N-d}3^{V_d}2^{-d},
\qquad
Z_{s,N}=
\sum_{r=1}^{s-1}3^{r-1}2^{-U_r}
+4\cdot3^{s-1}2^{-N}.
\tag{4.10}
\]

Condition on \(B_{N-d}=1\). The remaining \(s-2\) separators form a
uniform subset of \(N-2\) positions, so \(V_d\) is hypergeometric. Put
\(\pi_-=(s-2)/(N-2)\leq\pi\). For \(t\geq0\), expansion in falling
factorial moments gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[(1+t)^{V_d}\mid B_{N-d}=1]
&=
\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\binom{d-1}{j}t^j
\frac{(s-2)_{\underline j}}{(N-2)_{\underline j}}\\
&\leq
\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\binom{d-1}{j}(\pi_-t)^j
=(1+\pi_-t)^{d-1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.11}
\]
The falling-factorial ratio is zero when \(j>s-2\); otherwise each of its
factors is at most \(\pi_-\). Since \(\Pr(B_{N-d}=1)=\pi\), taking
\(t=3^\theta-1\) yields
\[
\mathbb E[B_{N-d}3^{\theta V_d}]
\leq
\pi[1+\pi(3^\theta-1)]^{d-1}.
\]

Choose
\[
\frac12+\varepsilon<\pi_*<\alpha_\theta.
\tag{4.12}
\]
For all sufficiently large \(N\), every
\(s\in\mathcal I_{N,\varepsilon}\) satisfies \(1<s<N\) and
\(\pi\leq\pi_*\). The quantity
\[
Q_*=
\frac{1+\pi_*(3^\theta-1)}{2^\theta}
<1
\tag{4.13}
\]
is subcritical precisely because \(\pi_*<\alpha_\theta\). By (4.10),
subadditivity of \(x^\theta\), and the preceding expectation bound,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{s,N}
\sum_{r=1}^{s-1}(3^{r-1}2^{-U_r})^\theta
&\leq
\sum_{d\geq1}2^{-\theta d}
\pi_*[1+\pi_*(3^\theta-1)]^{d-1}\\
&=
\frac{\pi_*}{2^\theta-1-\pi_*(3^\theta-1)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.14}
\]

By (4.4) and (4.12), after increasing the same fixed lower cutoff in
\(N\), every central pair satisfies \(s/N\leq(\log2)/(\log3)\).
The final term in (4.10) is then at most \(4/3\). Absorb the finitely many
remaining central pairs, including the deterministic cases \(s=1,N\), into
one constant. We obtain
\[
\mathbb E_{s,N}Z_{s,N}^\theta
\leq K_{\theta,\varepsilon}
\qquad
(N\geq1,\ s\in\mathcal I_{N,\varepsilon}).
\tag{4.15}
\]

Since \(L_{s,N}=2^{N-1}p_{N,s}\), one has
\[
\left(
\frac{3^s+\mathscr A_{\mathbf k}}{L_{s,N}}
\right)^\theta
=
2^\theta p_{N,s}^{-\theta}Z_{s,N}^\theta.
\tag{4.16}
\]
Lemma 3.3 and (4.15) imply
\[
\mathcal A_{s,N}(\theta)
\leq
(1+\theta)2^\theta
K_{\theta,\varepsilon}
p_{N,s}^{-\theta}.
\tag{4.17}
\]

It remains to sum the fractional powers of the symmetric-binomial masses.
The case \(N=1\) is immediate. For \(N\geq2\), set
\(a=1-\theta\in(0,1)\), and divide \(\{1,\ldots,N\}\) into the annuli
\[
I_j=
\left\{s:j\sqrt N\leq
\left|s-\frac{N+1}{2}\right|<(j+1)\sqrt N\right\}
\quad(j\geq0).
\]
Each annulus has at most \(4\sqrt N\) elements. Lemma 2.2 gives total
\(p_{N,s}\)-mass at most \(2e^{-2j^2}\) for \(j\geq1\), and at most one
for \(j=0\). Concavity on each annulus therefore gives
\[
\sum_{s=1}^Np_{N,s}^{1-\theta}
\leq
C_\theta N^{\theta/2}.
\tag{4.18}
\]
Indeed, its left side is at most
\[
(4\sqrt N)^\theta
\left(1+\sum_{j\geq1}(2e^{-2j^2})^{1-\theta}\right),
\]
and the series converges. Finally multiply (4.17) by \(p_{N,s}\), sum over
the central window, and use (4.18). This proves (4.7), after incorporating
the fixed factors into \(C_{\theta,\varepsilon}\).
\(\square\)

Theorem 4.1 completes the fixed-total part of the argument. Its bound concerns
one composition total \(N\); the next section must transfer it back to all
Collatz endpoint fibers in a shell and then use it against an arbitrary dense
target.

## 5. From endpoint moments to density transport

Theorem 4.1 controls a fixed total and a central range of composition lengths.
A Collatz shell mixes the possible first odd positions and also contains
noncentral cohorts. Section 5.1 recombines the central cohorts and proves that
the discarded source mass is exponentially small. Section 5.2 places endpoint
multiplicity and target density on one probability space and applies Hölder's
inequality. The output is a one-block theorem describing exactly how a
quantitative density exponent changes under pullback.

The central half-width \(\varepsilon\) is fixed before the target-density
exponent \(D\). This order matters because the pullback will be iterated at
exponents tending to zero. If the window changed with \(D\), both the
discarded-cohort rate and the moment constant would vary from stage to stage;
the theorem below would no longer provide one uniform recurrence. With
\(\varepsilon\) fixed, those constants remain fixed and the small-\(D\)
transport rate can be absorbed uniformly.

### 5.1. The central endpoint fibers

Fix \(M\) and \(1\leq s\leq M\). For a first-odd position \(u\), put
\(N=M-u\), and let \(f_{M,s,u}(y)\) be the number of starts in
\([2^M,2^{M+1})\) whose
parity word has weight \(s\), first odd position \(u\), and endpoint
\(T^M(n)=y\).

If two starts in this cohort have the same endpoint, (2.14) implies that
their corrections are congruent modulo \(3^s\). By (3.5), every endpoint
fiber therefore lies inside one residue fiber counted by
\(\nu_{s,N}\). If \(c_{s,N}(a)=L_{s,N}\nu_{s,N}(a)\), then, for every
\(q\geq1\),
\[
\sum_y f_{M,s,u}(y)^q
\leq
\sum_{a\bmod3^s}c_{s,N}(a)^q.
\tag{5.1}
\]
Indeed, inside one correction class the sum of the endpoint multiplicities is
at most \(c_{s,N}(a)\), and the \(q\)-th power of that sum dominates the
sum of the \(q\)-th powers.

Fix \(1/2<\theta<1\) and
\(0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0(\theta)\). Retain a cohort \(u\) only when
\[
s\in\mathcal I_{M-u,\varepsilon},
\tag{5.2}
\]
and let \(f^{\rm cen}_{M,s}(y)\) be the sum of
\(f_{M,s,u}(y)\) over the retained cohorts. Set
\(f^{\rm cen}_{M,0}(y)=0\), and define
\[
G_M^{\rm cen}(\theta,\varepsilon)
=
2^{-M}\sum_{s,y}f^{\rm cen}_{M,s}(y)
\left(
\frac{3^{s+1}f^{\rm cen}_{M,s}(y)}{\binom Ms}
\right)^\theta.
\tag{5.3}
\]

The first transfer step is to show that the fixed-total moment remains
polynomial after the retained first-odd-position cohorts are recombined.

### Lemma 5.1 (central endpoint moment)

There is \(C'_{\theta,\varepsilon}<\infty\) such that
\[
G_M^{\rm cen}(\theta,\varepsilon)
\leq
C'_{\theta,\varepsilon}(M+1)^{\theta/2}
\qquad(M\geq1).
\tag{5.4}
\]

#### Proof

For fixed \(M,s\), put
\[
\lambda_u=\frac{L_{s,M-u}}{\binom Ms}.
\]
The first-odd-position cohorts partition the weight-\(s\) words, so the
\(\lambda_u\) sum to one over all possible \(u\), and to at most one over
the retained \(u\). Adjoin a zero cohort carrying the missing weight. The
weights then sum to one, so weighted convexity and (5.1) give
\[
\left(\sum_{u\ {\rm retained}} f_{M,s,u}(y)\right)^{1+\theta}
\leq
\sum_{u\ {\rm retained}}
\lambda_u^{-\theta}f_{M,s,u}(y)^{1+\theta}.
\]
After summing over \(y\), (5.1) gives
\[
\sum_y f^{\rm cen}_{M,s}(y)^{1+\theta}
\leq
\binom Ms^\theta 3^{-s\theta}
\sum_{\substack{u\ {\rm retained}\\N=M-u}}
L_{s,N}\mathcal A_{s,N}(\theta).
\tag{5.5}
\]
Substitute this into (5.3). Since
\[
2^{-M}L_{s,M-u}
=
2^{-u-1}p_{M-u,s},
\]
Theorem 4.1 yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
G_M^{\rm cen}(\theta,\varepsilon)
&\leq
3^\theta
\sum_{u=0}^{M-1}2^{-u-1}
\sum_{s\in\mathcal I_{M-u,\varepsilon}}
p_{M-u,s}\mathcal A_{s,M-u}(\theta)\\
&\leq
3^\theta C_{\theta,\varepsilon}
\sum_{u=0}^{M-1}2^{-u-1}(M-u)^{\theta/2}\\
&\leq
3^\theta C_{\theta,\varepsilon}(M+1)^{\theta/2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
This is (5.4). \(\square\)

The moment estimate applies only to the central cohorts retained in (5.2).
The next lemma shows that their complement is too small to affect the
eventual pullback exponent.

### Lemma 5.2 (discarded cohorts)

There are \(C_\varepsilon,c_\varepsilon>0\), depending only on
\(\varepsilon\), such that the normalized source mass outside the retained
cohorts is at most
\[
C_\varepsilon e^{-c_\varepsilon M}.
\tag{5.6}
\]

#### Proof

At fixed \(u\), with \(N=M-u\), the normalized mass of all compositions is
\(2^{-u-1}\), and their number of parts has law \(J_N\) from (4.1).
For \(N\geq2\), Lemma 2.2 gives
\[
\Pr\left(
\left|J_N-\frac{N+1}{2}\right|>\varepsilon N
\right)
\leq
2e^{-2\varepsilon^2N}.
\tag{5.7}
\]
The all-even word has mass \(2^{-M}\). Hence the discarded mass is at most
\[
2^{-M}
+
\sum_{u=0}^{M-2}
2^{-u}e^{-2\varepsilon^2(M-u)}.
\]
Choose
\[
0<c_\varepsilon<\min(2\varepsilon^2,\log2).
\]
Each summand is bounded by
\[
e^{-c_\varepsilon M}
e^{-(\log2-c_\varepsilon)u},
\]
whose sum over \(u\) is a fixed geometric series. Also
\(2^{-M}\leq e^{-c_\varepsilon M}\). This proves (5.6).
\(\square\)

### 5.2. Pulling back a dense target

Define
\[
\begin{aligned}
\rho&=\frac{\theta}{1+\theta},
&\qquad b&=\frac{\theta}{2(1+\theta)},\\
\psi(D)&=-\log\left(\frac{1+3^{-D}}2\right),
&\qquad r_\theta(D)&=\rho\psi(D).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.8}
\]
Direct differentiation gives
\[
\psi'(D)=\frac{\log3}{3^D+1},
\qquad
\psi''(D)=
-\frac{(\log3)^2\,3^D}{(3^D+1)^2}<0.
\tag{5.9}
\]
Thus \(\psi\) is increasing and concave on \([0,1]\), with
\[
\psi(0)=0,
\qquad
\psi(D)\geq D\log(3/2)
\quad(0\leq D\leq1).
\tag{5.10}
\]

The remaining task is to test the endpoint multiplicity against the bad part
of an arbitrary target. A reference probability measure places the target
density and the endpoint moment on the same finite space. Hölder's inequality
then combines them directly; Lemma 5.2 contributes the separate discarded
cohort error.

### Theorem 5.3 (central endpoint pullback)

Fix \(1/2<\theta<1\) and
\(0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0(\theta)\). There is
\(D_{\theta,\varepsilon}>0\) such that, for every \(0<\zeta<1\), there is
\(K_{\theta,\varepsilon,\zeta}>0\) with the following property: whenever
\(0<D\leq D_{\theta,\varepsilon}\) and \(S\) is \((C,D)\)-dense,
\(\operatorname{Pull}(S)\) is
\[
\left(
K_{\theta,\varepsilon,\zeta}
\left(1+C^\rho\right)D^{-b},
\frac{\zeta r_\theta(D)}{\log2}
\right)\text{-dense}.
\tag{5.11}
\]
All constants in (5.11) are independent of \(C,D,S\), the dyadic shell,
and every later use of the theorem.

Consequently, if
\[
0<\chi<
\Lambda(\theta)
:=
a_0\frac{\theta}{1+\theta},
\tag{5.12}
\]
then one may choose \(0<\zeta<1\) and a further cutoff
\(D_{\rm lin}=D_{\rm lin}(\theta,\varepsilon,\zeta,\chi)>0\) so that
the density exponent in (5.11) is at least \(\chi D\) whenever
\(0<D\leq D_{\rm lin}\).

Thus, after \(\theta,\varepsilon,\chi\) and a compatible \(\zeta\) are
fixed, \(K_{\theta,\varepsilon,\zeta}\) and \(D_{\rm lin}\) do not change
when later iterations send \(D\) to zero.

#### Proof

Let \(S\) be \((C,D)\)-dense, and fix a shell
\([2^M,2^{M+1})\). The shell \(M=0\) contains one integer and is absorbed
by the final density constant, so assume \(M\geq1\). Put
\[
q=1+\theta,
\qquad
w_s=2^{-M}\binom Ms,
\qquad
Y_s=3^{s+1},
\]
and, on the finite set of pairs \((s,y)\) with \(0\leq s\leq M\) and
\(1\leq y\leq Y_s\), define
\[
\mu(s,y)=\frac{w_s}{Y_s},
\qquad
h(s,y)=\frac{Y_sf^{\rm cen}_{M,s}(y)}{\binom Ms}.
\tag{5.13}
\]
Here endpoints outside their actual range simply have multiplicity zero.
The binomial theorem and (5.3) give the exact identities
\[
\sum_{s,y}\mu(s,y)=1,
\qquad
\int h^q\,d\mu=G_M^{\rm cen}(\theta,\varepsilon).
\tag{5.14}
\]

Let \(E=\{(s,y):y\notin S\}\). By the definition of \((C,D)\)-density,
the number of bad \(y\leq Y_s\) is at most \(CY_s^{1-D}\). Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu(E)
&\leq
C\sum_{s=0}^Mw_sY_s^{-D}\\
&=
C3^{-D}\left(\frac{1+3^{-D}}2\right)^M
=C3^{-D}e^{-\psi(D)M}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.15}
\]
Moreover, \(\int_Eh\,d\mu\) is exactly the normalized source mass in
retained central cohorts whose endpoint is outside \(S\). Hölder's inequality
with conjugate exponents \(q\) and \(q/\theta\) gives
\[
\int_Eh\,d\mu
\leq
\left(G_M^{\rm cen}(\theta,\varepsilon)\right)^{1/q}
\mu(E)^\rho.
\tag{5.16}
\]
Using Lemma 5.1, \(3^{-D}\leq1\), and \(r_\theta(D)=\rho\psi(D)\), the
right side is at most
\[
K_{\theta,\varepsilon}C^\rho
(M+1)^b e^{-r_\theta(D)M}.
\tag{5.17}
\]
Adding the discarded cohorts from Lemma 5.2 gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\#\{n\in[2^M,2^{M+1}):T^M(n)\notin S\}}{2^M}
&\leq C_\varepsilon e^{-c_\varepsilon M}\\
&\quad+K_{\theta,\varepsilon}C^\rho
(M+1)^b e^{-r_\theta(D)M}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.18}
\]

Choose \(D_{\theta,\varepsilon}\in(0,1]\) so that
\[
r_\theta(D_{\theta,\varepsilon})
\leq
\min\{c_\varepsilon,\log(4/3)\}.
\tag{5.19}
\]
This choice is possible because \(r_\theta\) is continuous, increasing, and
vanishes at zero. It depends only on \(\theta,\varepsilon\). For
\(0<D\leq D_{\theta,\varepsilon}\), the first term in (5.18) is bounded
by a constant multiple of \(e^{-r_\theta(D)M}\). Since
\(1+C^\rho\geq1\) and \(D^{-b}\geq1\), it can be absorbed into the same
final constant as the central contribution.

We next absorb the polynomial factor without changing its dependence on
\(D\). For \(B\geq0\), \(0<u\leq1\), and \(M\geq1\),
\[
(M+1)^B e^{-uM}
\leq
K_{B,\zeta}u^{-B}e^{-\zeta uM},
\tag{5.20}
\]
where \(K_{B,\zeta}\) is independent of \(u,M\). Indeed,
\[
(M+1)^Bu^Be^{-(1-\zeta)uM}
\leq
(2uM)^Be^{-(1-\zeta)uM},
\]
and the right side has a finite supremum as a function of \(uM\geq0\).
Apply (5.20) with \(B=b\) and \(u=r_\theta(D)\).
By (5.10),
\[
r_\theta(D)
\geq
\frac{\theta\log(3/2)}{1+\theta}D.
\tag{5.21}
\]
Thus (5.18) becomes
\[
\frac{
\#\{n\in[2^M,2^{M+1}):T^M(n)\notin S\}
}{2^M}
\leq
K_{\theta,\varepsilon,\zeta}
\left(1+C^\rho\right)D^{-b}
e^{-\zeta r_\theta(D)M}.
\tag{5.22}
\]

The fixed-rate part of Lemma 2.1 applies because, by (5.19),
\(\zeta r_\theta(D)<\log2\). Its geometric-series factor is at most
\[
\frac{2}{2e^{-\zeta r_\theta(D)}-1}
\leq4.
\tag{5.23}
\]
This proves (5.11), after enlarging
\(K_{\theta,\varepsilon,\zeta}\).

Finally fix \(\chi<\Lambda(\theta)\) and choose \(\zeta<1\) so that
\(\chi<\zeta\Lambda(\theta)\). Since \(\psi'\) is decreasing and
\(\psi(0)=0\), concavity gives \(\psi(D)\geq D\psi'(D)\). Hence (5.9)
gives
\[
\frac{\zeta r_\theta(D)}{D\log2}
\geq
\frac{\zeta\theta\log3}
{(1+\theta)\log2\,(3^D+1)}.
\tag{5.24}
\]
The right side tends to \(\zeta\Lambda(\theta)>\chi\) as
\(D\downarrow0\). More explicitly, (5.24) is at least \(\chi\) whenever
\[
0<D\leq
\frac{
\log\left(
\dfrac{\zeta\theta\log3}
{(1+\theta)\chi\log2}
-1
\right)
}{\log3}.
\tag{5.25}
\]
The quantity inside the logarithm is greater than one by the choice of
\(\zeta\). Define \(D_{\rm lin}\) to be the minimum of this bound, \(1\),
and \(D_{\theta,\varepsilon}\). This proves (5.12). \(\square\)

This is the interface needed for iteration: for sufficiently small \(D\), a
pullback multiplies the density exponent by any fixed
\(\chi<\Lambda(\theta)\), while its density constant grows by a contractive
power of \(C\) and only polynomially in \(D^{-1}\). The refined prefactor
improves finite-stage bookkeeping, but the limiting slope \(\Lambda(\theta)\)
is unchanged; in particular, it does not enlarge \(\delta_0\).

## 6. Endpoint iteration

The pullback theorem controls density from one block to the next, but it does
not itself say that a block contracts. We therefore need a fixed dense seed
set on which the endpoint map decreases scale. Intersecting that seed with
successive pullbacks ensures both that the current block contracts and that
its endpoint remains eligible for the remaining blocks. Lemma 6.1 constructs
the seed set; Proposition 6.2 combines the resulting orbit recursion with the
density recursion.

### 6.1. A dense one-block contraction

Define
\[
F(n)=T^{\lfloor\log_2n\rfloor}(n).
\tag{6.1}
\]
Fix \(a_0<r<1\), and put
\[
\eta_r=\frac{r-a_0}{\log_2 3},
\qquad
\mathcal E_r=\{n\geq1:F(n)\leq3n^r\}.
\tag{6.2}
\]

The heuristic \(s\approx M/2\) is not enough for recursion: we need one fixed
contraction set whose complement has a quantitative shell bound. Exact parity
uniformity from Proposition 2.3 and the binomial tail in Lemma 2.2 provide it
for every fixed \(r>a_0\).

### Lemma 6.1 (fixed contraction set)

There are constants \(C_r,D_r>0\) such that \(\mathcal E_r\) is
\((C_r,D_r)\)-dense.

#### Proof

Fix a shell \([2^M,2^{M+1})\), and let \(s\) be the number of odd steps
among the first \(M\) iterates. The case \(M=0\) is immediate. For
\(M\geq1\), if
\[
s\leq\frac M2+\eta_rM,
\tag{6.3}
\]
then Corollary 2.5 gives
\[
F(n)<3^{s+1}
\leq
3\cdot2^{(a_0+\eta_r\log_2 3)M}
=3\cdot2^{rM}
\leq3n^r.
\tag{6.4}
\]
By Proposition 2.3, the parity words are uniform on \(\{0,1\}^M\).
Lemma 2.2 therefore gives
\[
\frac{\#(\mathcal E_r^c\cap[2^M,2^{M+1}))}{2^M}
\leq
2e^{-2\eta_r^2M}.
\tag{6.5}
\]
Choose
\[
0<\gamma_r<\min\{2\eta_r^2,\log2\}.
\]
Then (6.5) implies (2.2) with \(K=2\) and \(\gamma=\gamma_r\).
Lemma 2.1 proves the claim, for example with
\[
C_r=\frac4{2e^{-\gamma_r}-1},
\qquad
D_r=\frac{\gamma_r}{\log2}.
\tag{6.6}
\]
\(\square\)

Only the terminal odd count is used here. No control of intermediate
iterates inside a block is needed for an orbit-minimum theorem.

### 6.2. The recursive endpoint sets

Set \(K_{\rm blk}=3\). With \(r\) fixed as above, define
\[
\mathcal S_1=\mathcal E_r,
\qquad
\mathcal S_{j+1}
=
\mathcal E_r\cap\operatorname{Pull}(\mathcal S_j).
\tag{6.7}
\]
If \(n\in\mathcal S_R\), put
\[
n_0=n,
\qquad
n_{i+1}=F(n_i)
\quad(0\leq i<R).
\tag{6.8}
\]
Induction in (6.7) gives \(n_i\in\mathcal E_r\) for \(0\leq i<R\).
Consequently
\[
n_i
\leq
K_{\rm blk}^{1+r+\cdots+r^{i-1}}n^{r^i}
\leq
K_{\rm blk}^{1/(1-r)}n^{r^i}.
\tag{6.9}
\]
Each \(n_i\) is an actual Collatz iterate of \(n\). More precisely, if
\[
\tau_R
=
\sum_{i=0}^{R-1}\lfloor\log_2n_i\rfloor,
\tag{6.10}
\]
then \(n_R=T^{\tau_R}(n)\), and (6.9) gives
\[
\tau_R
\leq
\frac{\log n}{(1-r)\log2}
+
\frac{R\log K_{\rm blk}}{(1-r)\log2}.
\tag{6.11}
\]

Equations (6.7)--(6.11) settle the deterministic part: membership in
\(\mathcal S_R\) gives an actual Collatz iterate with the required size and
clock. It remains to show that \(R\) may grow like \(\log\log n\) without the
exceptional set becoming too large. The next proposition isolates that
calculation from the specific pullback theorem.

### Proposition 6.2 (endpoint bootstrap)

Fix \(a_0<r<1\). Suppose there are constants
\[
0<\chi<a_0,
\qquad
0<\varrho<1,
\qquad
B\geq0,
\qquad
K_{\rm L},D_c>0
\tag{6.12}
\]
such that, whenever \(0<D\leq D_c\), pullback sends every
\((C,D)\)-dense set to a set with density parameters
\[
(C,D)
\longmapsto
\bigl(K_{\rm L}(1+C^\varrho)D^{-B},\chi D\bigr).
\tag{6.13}
\]
For every \(\omega>0\) satisfying
\[
\omega\log(1/\chi)<1,
\tag{6.14}
\]
define, on the shell \([2^M,2^{M+1})\),
\[
R_M=\left\lceil\omega\log(M+4)\right\rceil.
\tag{6.15}
\]
Put
\[
\mathcal S^{(\omega)}
=
\bigcup_{M\geq0}
\left(\mathcal S_{R_M}\cap[2^M,2^{M+1})\right).
\]
Then the shell exceptional proportion outside \(\mathcal S_{R_M}\) is at
most
\[
2\exp\left(-c(M+4)^{1-\gamma}\right)
\tag{6.16}
\]
for all sufficiently large \(M\), where \(c>0\) and
\[
\gamma=\omega\log(1/\chi)<1.
\tag{6.17}
\]
The set \(\mathcal S^{(\omega)}\) has natural density one. On this set,
\[
\log n_{R_M}
\leq
\frac{\log K_{\rm blk}}{1-r}
+
(M+1)\log2\,(M+4)^{-\omega\log(1/r)},
\tag{6.18}
\]
and the witnessing time satisfies (6.11) with \(R=R_M\).

In particular, every
\[
0<\delta<
\frac{\log(1/r)}{\log(1/\chi)}
\tag{6.19}
\]
can be realized by a choice of \(\omega\) for which
\[
n_{R_M}
\leq
\exp((\log n)^{1-\delta})
\tag{6.20}
\]
on all sufficiently large retained shells.

#### Proof

Choose
\[
0<D_1\leq\min\{D_r,D_c,1\}.
\]
After weakening the exponent in Lemma 6.1 to \(D_1\), put \(C_1=C_r\).
Then \(\mathcal E_r\) is \((C_1,D_1)\)-dense. Inductively, the sets
in (6.7) are \((C_j,D_j)\)-dense with
\[
D_{j+1}=\chi D_j,
\qquad
C_{j+1}
\leq
K_{\rm L}(1+C_j^\varrho)D_j^{-B}+C_r.
\tag{6.21}
\]
Indeed, \(D_j\leq D_1\leq D_c\), so pullback is applicable, and
\(\chi D_j\leq D_1\leq D_r\), so intersection with \(\mathcal E_r\)
preserves the exponent \(\chi D_j\). Thus
\[
D_j=D_1\chi^{j-1}.
\tag{6.22}
\]

Put \(u_j=\log(C_j+2)\). Since
\(1+C_j^\varrho\leq2(C_j+2)^\varrho\), \(D_j\leq1\), and
\((C_j+2)^\varrho D_j^{-B}\geq1\), (6.21) gives
\[
u_{j+1}
\leq
\varrho u_j+L_0+B(j-1)\log(\chi^{-1}),
\qquad
L_0=\log(2K_{\rm L}+C_r+2)+B\log(D_1^{-1}).
\]
Iteration yields, for \(R\geq2\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
u_R
&\leq
\varrho^{R-1}u_1
+\sum_{j=1}^{R-1}\varrho^{R-1-j}
\left(L_0+B(j-1)\log(\chi^{-1})\right)\\
&\leq
u_1+\frac{L_0+B(R-2)\log(\chi^{-1})}{1-\varrho}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.23}
\]
In particular,
\[
\log(C_R+2)=O(R).
\tag{6.24}
\]

From (6.15) and (6.22),
\[
D_{R_M}
\geq
D_1(M+4)^{-\omega\log(1/\chi)}.
\tag{6.25}
\]
The \((C_{R_M},D_{R_M})\)-density bound implies that the exceptional
proportion on shell \(M\) is at most
\[
2C_{R_M}e^{-(\log2)D_{R_M}M}.
\tag{6.26}
\]
Equations (6.14), (6.24), and (6.25) give
\[
\log(2C_{R_M})-(\log2)D_{R_M}M
\leq
O(\log M)-c_1M^{1-\gamma}.
\tag{6.27}
\]
After decreasing the positive constant and increasing the fixed starting
shell, this proves (6.16). Lemma 2.1, part 2, then shows that the union of
the retained shell sets has natural density one.

Equation (6.18) follows from (6.9), because
\[
r^{R_M}
\leq
(M+4)^{-\omega\log(1/r)}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\log n<(M+1)\log2.
\]
If
\[
\delta<\omega\log(1/r),
\tag{6.28}
\]
then the right side of (6.18) is eventually at most
\((\log n)^{1-\delta}\). Indeed, after division by that quantity, its
second term is
\[
O\left(M^{\delta-\omega\log(1/r)}\right)=o(1),
\]
and its fixed first term also tends to zero. Thus (6.20) holds. Conditions
(6.14) and (6.28)
admit a common \(\omega\) exactly when (6.19) holds. \(\square\)

Proposition 6.2 reduces the final theorem to a scalar compatibility condition:
the endpoint must contract at rate \(r\), while the density exponent survives
each pullback at rate \(\chi\). Section 7 chooses these parameters using the
slope supplied by Theorem 5.3.

## 7. Proof of Theorem 1.1

The arithmetic and iteration arguments are now complete. Theorem 5.3 permits
every pullback slope below \(\Lambda(\theta)\), while Proposition 6.2 converts
such a slope and a contraction exponent \(r\) into a stretched-logarithmic
descent exponent. What remains is to choose the parameters with strict
margins, pass to the limiting endpoint, and retain the exceptional-count and
time bounds.

For \(1/2<\theta<1\), define
\[
\Lambda(\theta)
=a_0\frac{\theta}{1+\theta},
\qquad
\delta_*(\theta)
=
\frac{\log(1/a_0)}{\log(1/\Lambda(\theta))}.
\tag{7.1}
\]
The first number is the limiting transport slope from Theorem 5.3; the second
is the endpoint exponent supplied by Proposition 6.2 at that slope.

The limiting value of this scalar expression determines the range stated in
Theorem 1.1.

### Lemma 7.1 (endpoint limit)

The function \(\delta_*\) is strictly increasing on \((1/2,1)\), and
\[
\lim_{\theta\uparrow1}\delta_*(\theta)
=
\frac{\log(1/a_0)}{\log(2/a_0)}
=\delta_0.
\tag{7.2}
\]

#### Proof

The function \(\theta\mapsto\theta/(1+\theta)\) is strictly increasing.
Hence \(\Lambda(\theta)\) is strictly increasing and lies in \((0,1)\);
therefore \(\log(1/\Lambda(\theta))\) is positive and strictly decreasing.
This proves the monotonicity of \(\delta_*\). Since
\(\Lambda(\theta)\to a_0/2\) as \(\theta\uparrow1\), continuity gives
(7.2). \(\square\)

The strict endpoint reflects a boundary of the present moment argument. As
\(\theta\uparrow1\), the admissible half-width \(\varepsilon_0(\theta)\) in
(4.5) tends to zero, and at \(\theta=1\) the geometric ratio in (4.14)
reaches one at the central value \(\pi=1/2\). Thus Theorem 4.1 supplies
neither a fixed central window nor a subcritical estimate at the endpoint.
This does not rule out attaining or exceeding \(\delta_0\) by a different
estimate.

We now choose the parameters in the order required by their dependencies:
first the moment order and central window, then the contraction and pullback
rates, and finally the number of blocks.

### Proof of Theorem 1.1

Fix \(0<\delta<\delta_0\),
\(0<\sigma<1-\delta/\delta_0\), and \(c>c_*\). Put
\[
d=\frac{\delta}{1-\sigma}<\delta_0.
\tag{7.3}
\]
By Lemma 7.1 choose
\[
\frac12<\theta<1
\quad\text{such that}\quad
d<\delta_*(\theta),
\tag{7.4}
\]
and then fix \(0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_0(\theta)\). Since the
inequality in (7.4) is strict, choose \(0<\chi<\Lambda(\theta)\) close
enough to \(\Lambda(\theta)\), and then \(r>a_0\) close enough to
\(a_0\), that
\[
d<\frac{\log(1/r)}{\log(1/\chi)},
\qquad
\frac1{(1-r)\log2}<c.
\tag{7.5}
\]
The two requirements can be met simultaneously because their limiting
values at \(r=a_0\) are strict by (7.4) and by \(c>c_*\).

Choose \(\zeta\) and \(D_{\rm lin}\) from Theorem 5.3. Its linear-rate
consequence supplies Proposition 6.2 with
\[
\varrho=\frac{\theta}{1+\theta},
\qquad
B=\frac{\theta}{2(1+\theta)},
\qquad
K_{\rm L}=K_{\theta,\varepsilon,\zeta},
\qquad
D_c=D_{\rm lin}.
\tag{7.6}
\]
Set
\[
\omega=\frac{1-\sigma}{\log(1/\chi)}.
\tag{7.7}
\]
Then
\[
\omega\log(1/\chi)=1-\sigma<1,
\qquad
\delta<\omega\log(1/r).
\tag{7.8}
\]
Proposition 6.2 therefore gives retained shell sets whose exceptional
proportion is at most
\[
2\exp(-\kappa_0(M+4)^\sigma)
\tag{7.9}
\]
for some \(\kappa_0>0\) and all sufficiently large \(M\). On those same sets,
(6.18) and (7.8) give
\[
n_{R_M}=T^{\tau_{R_M}}(n)
\leq\exp((\log n)^{1-\delta})
\tag{7.10}
\]
eventually. Moreover, (6.11), (6.15), and (7.5) give
\[
\tau_{R_M}
\leq
\frac{\log n}{(1-r)\log2}+O_r(\log\log n)
<c\log n
\tag{7.11}
\]
on all sufficiently large retained shells.

Thus the shell estimate (7.9) bounds the failure of the two conditions in
(1.3) jointly. Apply Lemma 2.1, part 2, with \(A=2\). Since its resulting
prefactor is \(2A+1=5\), there are
\(a_{\delta,\sigma,c}>0\) and \(X_{\delta,\sigma,c}\) such that
\[
\begin{aligned}
&\#\bigl\{1\leq n\leq X:\nexists k\in\mathbb N_0\text{ such that}\\
&\hspace{38mm} k<c\log n\ \text{and}\
T^k(n)\leq\exp((\log n)^{1-\delta})\bigr\}\\
&\hspace{20mm}\leq
5X\exp\left(-a_{\delta,\sigma,c}(\log X)^\sigma\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.12}
\]
for every \(X\geq X_{\delta,\sigma,c}\). This is (1.3).

For any fixed admissible \(\sigma,c\), the right side of (7.12) is
\(o(X)\). The set of integers admitting the displayed witness therefore has
natural density one, and every such witness also proves (1.2). This proves
part 1.

It remains only to certify the convenient decimal in (1.4). Since
\[
c_*=\frac1{(1-a_0)\log2}=\frac2{\log(4/3)},
\tag{7.13}
\]
putting \(x=1/7\) in
\[
\log\frac{1+x}{1-x}
=2\sum_{j\geq0}\frac{x^{2j+1}}{2j+1}
\]
and retaining the first two positive terms gives
\[
\log\frac43>\frac{296}{1029},
\qquad
c_*<\frac{1029}{148}<\frac{6953}{1000}.
\tag{7.14}
\]
The last inequality is the integer comparison
\(1{,}029{,}000<1{,}029{,}044\). Thus \(c=6.953\) is allowed in part 2.
\(\square\)

The shortcut clock also gives a concise statement for the unaccelerated map.
Let
\[
\operatorname{Col}(n)=
\begin{cases}
n/2,&n\text{ even},\\
3n+1,&n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
\tag{7.15}
\]

### Corollary 7.2 (raw Collatz clock)

Fix \(0<\delta<\delta_0\),
\(0<\sigma<1-\delta/\delta_0\), and
\[
\widehat c>\frac3{\log(4/3)}.
\tag{7.16}
\]
There are \(a>0\) and \(X_0\) such that, for every \(X\geq X_0\), the
number of \(n\leq X\) for which no
\(\ell\in\mathbb N_0\) with \(\ell<\widehat c\log n\) satisfies
\[
\operatorname{Col}^{\ell}(n)
\leq\exp((\log n)^{1-\delta})
\]
is at most \(5X\exp(-a(\log X)^\sigma)\).

#### Proof

Choose \(c>c_*\) sufficiently close to \(c_*\) that
\[
c\left(1+\frac{\log2}{\log3}\right)-\frac1{\log3}
<\widehat c.
\tag{7.17}
\]
Suppose the shortcut witness in Theorem 1.1 uses \(k\) steps, contains \(s\)
odd steps, and lands at \(y\). The corresponding raw witness has length
\(\ell=k+s\). If \(k=0\), the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise,
Proposition 2.4 gives
\[
3^sn\leq2^ky,
\qquad
\ell\leq
k\left(1+\frac{\log2}{\log3}\right)
+\frac{\log y-\log n}{\log3}.
\]
Since \(k<c\log n\) and
\(\log y\leq(\log n)^{1-\delta}=o(\log n)\), (7.17) gives
\(\ell<\widehat c\log n\) for all sufficiently large retained \(n\).
At \(c=c_*\), the left side of (7.17) is exactly
\(3/\log(4/3)\). The exceptional count is inherited from (1.3).
\(\square\)

## 8. Scope and reproducibility

The proof is complete at this point. We finish by separating the theorem from
stronger Collatz conclusions that it does not imply and by recording the
formal artifact independently of the manuscript proof.

### 8.1. Scope and comparison

Theorem 1.1 is compatible with exceptional divergent trajectories and with
hypothetical nontrivial cycles. If an orbit enters a fixed cycle with minimum
\(m\), then its orbit minimum is at most \(m\), so all sufficiently large
points in that basin already satisfy (1.2). The theorem therefore gives no
cycle exclusion and no density estimate for such basins.

Tao [8] proves the stronger terminal-threshold statement with an arbitrary
function tending to infinity in logarithmic density. The concurrent
preprints [1, 6] obtain arbitrary-diverging-threshold statements in natural
density through bridges built on Tao's framework. Theorem 1.1 has a less
general terminal threshold; its separate features are the self-contained
fixed-total endpoint argument, the arbitrary-target transport theorem, the
prescribed stretched-logarithmic family, the counting-cutoff estimate (1.3),
and the smaller explicit clock at that target.

The later first-passage preprint [7] gives stronger landing conclusions,
including polylogarithmic descent, through a different architecture. It does
not replace Theorem 5.3 as an arbitrary-target operator. The two papers thus
have different theorem surfaces: the present one proves transport for every
quantitatively dense target and then gives descent as an application.

The fixed-power statements preceding this paper do not by themselves give
the prescribed rate in (1.2). A density-one diagonalization over all fixed
powers can produce some unspecified exponent \(\varepsilon(n)\to0\), but its
decay depends on untracked onset thresholds. It does not imply
\(\varepsilon(n)=(\log n)^{-\delta}\), does not identify one uniform
density-one set at that prescribed rate, and does not give (1.3).
